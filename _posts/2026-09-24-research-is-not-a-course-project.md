Many undergraduate and master’s students are interested in gaining research experience, and some begin with the ambition to publish a paper. Programs such as Rice University’s [SURF](https://ouri.rice.edu/research-programs/surf) offer an opportunity to take that first step.

In working with students, however, I have noticed some recurring patterns in how beginners approach research. I recognize some of these beliefs because I held them myself when I started my PhD. I learned the hard way how they could lead to wasted time and effort.

I am writing this article to help beginners avoid these mistakes early on. Understanding how academic research and research apprenticeship work is essential, but students without an academic family background or prior research experience may have little access to this knowledge.

## 1. The wrong mental model: research as a course project

A beginner’s expectations may look something like this:

- My advisor knows a method that will work.
- If I follow the proposed steps, I will obtain the expected result.
- I can estimate when those steps will be finished and arrange my other commitments around that timeline.
- Taking on several projects will improve my chances that at least one succeeds.
- My advisor will tell me what to do next and keep the project moving.
- Committing too strongly to one direction will limit my future choices.

These beliefs are understandable. Coursework and many engineering projects ask you to solve a defined problem using established methods. Students and even experienced engineers may therefore assume that research has a similarly predictable relationship between effort and results.

Academic research also involves solving problems, but the goal is to discover new knowledge. It is closer to a treasure hunt: you have clues about where to look, but you still need to find out which ones lead somewhere. You may need to determine how to formulate the problem, whether a proposed method works, why it works, and whether the findings apply beyond the cases you tested.

The research plan itself contains assumptions you need to test. Your advisor’s experience can help you choose where to begin, but neither of you can know in advance how the investigation will turn out.

Even if you follow the plan carefully, you may find that the approach does not work—or discover something unexpected that points to a more promising direction. Either can be a reason to change the plan. What you learn along the way helps you decide what to try next, even if it does not immediately lead to a publishable result.

## 2. Your responsibility goes beyond carrying out your advisor’s plan

At first, research may feel like a series of assignments. Your advisor gives you tasks, you complete them, and you report the results. When the early tasks go well, it is easy to think:

> “These tasks took two days. I have finished this week’s research work, so I can spend the remaining time on courses, assignments, and other priorities.”

Other commitments are normal. The assumption worth questioning is that completing the assigned tasks fulfills your responsibility to the research.

Eventually, an approach may fail. Your advisor asks for changes and additional work outside the original plan. You may think:

> “You chose the approach and told me what to do. I did it carefully and spent considerable time on it. If it doesn’t work, I have fulfilled my part. Why should I keep paying for your mistaken advice?”

I remember feeling this way early in my PhD, and I have seen other students struggle with the same frustration.

This reaction assumes that the advisor is responsible for choosing the approach and interpreting the results, while the student handles execution. But that leaves the advisor as the only person questioning assumptions and deciding what comes next. Your advisor can be wrong, and the resulting delay still affects your publication and progress.

Research apprenticeship should gradually prepare you to share those responsibilities. This expectation often remains implicit. Even “take ownership” can sound like “complete your assignments reliably” when the advisor means that you should also help examine and shape the plan.

For example, an advisor may recommend a method or simulation tool because several published papers report promising results. Once you begin using it, you may discover that the method relies on an assumption your problem does not satisfy, or that the simulator cannot model a feature you need. The recommendation was reasonable, but its suitability could only be established through actual work.

Taking ownership starts with understanding the ultimate research question. That begins before you carry out the suggested work. Understand why the problem matters and what the approach is supposed to accomplish, then keep asking questions:

- What could go wrong? What assumptions or limitations should we examine?
- What evidence would support the idea, and what would show that it fails?
- What should I read to understand the problem better? Are there other ways to address it?

Discuss these questions with your advisor and investigate them yourself. A recommendation can be well informed and still depend on assumptions neither of you has tested.

I once described an optimization method to a friend who was an experienced researcher. I was convinced it would work, and he was initially excited too. A day later, he returned with a Jupyter notebook showing that it did not. The test required only sophomore-level skills. The scientific judgment lay in recognizing which assumption to challenge and how a simple experiment could settle it, saving us weeks of pursuing a dead end. 

That is a productive research habit: take promising ideas seriously while looking for reasons they might fail. As you gain experience, your contribution should increasingly include testing the reasoning behind a plan, interpreting what you find, and proposing what to do next.

## 3. Work in focused sprints, with time to reconsider

Research should be organized around resolving uncertainties and using what you learn to decide what to do next. This is why I find it useful to work in focused sprints, with reflection and exploration between them: each sprint tests something consequential, and its result helps determine the next step.

Each sprint should prioritize an uncertainty whose resolution could change what you do next. If a method seems promising, begin with the smallest experiment that can meaningfully test its critical assumption before building the full system or running a large study. A small test may not establish that the whole approach will succeed, but it can expose a flaw that makes further investment premature. Discovering that early is a substantial gain.

You and your advisor may assess these uncertainties differently. Working directly with the method, code, or data, you may notice a questionable assumption your advisor has overlooked or considers settled. Make it explicit, test it early, and report what the evidence shows. This gives your advisor a chance to revise the plan before more effort depends on that assumption. Identifying what needs testing is part of your contribution as a researcher.

The point of a sprint is to reach informative evidence quickly. It does not require continuous exhaustion. Reflection is essential because intense effort can otherwise keep you pursuing an assumption that no longer deserves your confidence.

This is also why initiative matters early. Some challenges become visible only when you attempt the work. The earlier you encounter those obstacles, the earlier you can start finding a route through or around them.

A research plan should evolve through these cycles. You commit seriously to the investigation while updating your understanding of what it requires.

I learned the opposite approach the hard way. I treated research like a marathon on a known route: estimate the distance, choose a manageable pace, and distribute my effort until the expected finish. That felt reasonable because I assumed the proposed route would work. I organized tasks around completing the plan efficiently, rather than finding out which assumptions could invalidate it.

These priorities can produce very different schedules. An execution-focused plan might put a difficult experiment after weeks of preparation and implementation. An uncertainty-focused plan asks whether a simplified version of that experiment should come first, because failure would change what is worth building. Delaying that test can mean carefully completing work that later has to be discarded. Testing it early gives you more time—and more remaining capacity—to find a viable direction.

## 4. Explore, then give a direction a serious chance

Because research is uncertain, diversifying across several projects can look like a sensible strategy: if one fails, perhaps another will succeed.

But your projects compete for the same limited attention. Each may need sustained effort before its central difficulty becomes clear, followed by further effort to resolve it. Dividing your time can leave every project short of the depth it requires.

A funding agency can spread risk across many independent teams. An established researcher may have collaborators, experience, and resources that support several directions. An individual beginner usually cannot reproduce that arrangement simply by joining more projects. Even faculty early in their careers face serious limits on how widely they can distribute their attention.

Explore possible directions early. Then select a problem and give it a serious chance.

Publishing at a strong venue is a little like competing in the Olympics: you are testing your work against contributions from talented, dedicated people around the world. Success takes sustained focus and preparation. Giving several projects a fraction of your attention may leave you poorly prepared to compete in any of them.

Publication also involves a threshold. You can learn a great deal and complete substantial work without yet producing a contribution that a reputable venue will accept. There is no reliable conversion from hours spent or tasks completed to an accepted paper.

That makes sustained commitment important. It does not mean persisting indefinitely with a disproven approach. Good judgment includes changing methods and sometimes deciding that a project should end. The aim is to give a promising direction enough attention to discover what it can become.

## 5. Expect your advisor’s advice to change

The execution-plan mentality can make the advisor relationship unnecessarily painful.

You may hear a recommendation as, “Follow these steps, and you will get a publishable result.” Your advisor may mean, “Given what we currently know, this is a promising route to investigate.”

When the method fails or the recommendation changes, you may feel that the goalposts have moved. You did what was asked, but now there is more work—or different work—to do. You may wonder whether the original guidance was careless or whether your effort was wasted.

Those feelings are understandable. But a changed recommendation can be the consequence of learning something neither of you knew before.

Expect advice and plans to change, sometimes frequently. Ask what prompted the revision. Explain what you found and help work out its implications. These discussions should gradually make you more capable of judging the direction yourself.

This does not remove your responsibility to evaluate the mentorship you receive. An advisor should help you understand important changes and respond constructively to evidence. Denying earlier advice, assigning blame unfairly, or repeatedly changing direction without explanation deserves separate attention.

The right expectation is that the route will evolve, and that you and your advisor should be able to discuss why.

## 6. Take ownership—and understand how trust develops

A research apprenticeship involves mutual commitment. Your advisor contributes experience, judgment, and guidance. You contribute sustained engagement and, increasingly, independent thinking.

Do not expect your advisor to manage every day of the project. Come to discussions ready to explain what you tried, what happened, what you think it means, and what you propose to do next. When you need help, identify the obstacle as clearly as you can.

Ownership does not mean knowing the answer before asking a question. It means taking responsibility for helping the investigation move forward.

There is also a relational dynamic that beginners may not recognize.

While you are deciding whether your advisor is helpful, your advisor is forming impressions of your engagement. Do you follow through? Do you investigate suggestions seriously? Do you communicate when something fails? Does a discussion lead to a thoughtful next attempt?

Those impressions can affect the attention your project receives. When your advisor sees that their guidance leads to useful work, they may become more invested. Their increased involvement can then help you make better progress, strengthening the relationship further.

The reverse can happen as well. You feel unsure and hesitate. Your advisor interprets hesitation as limited interest and becomes less involved. You experience that reduced attention as rejection and become even less confident.

The hardest part is that these judgments may remain unspoken. Some professors give direct feedback. Others hesitate because they want to avoid conflict, worry about how criticism will be received, or are cautious about professional and reputational consequences. A student may notice declining support without ever receiving a clear explanation.

Do not assume silence means satisfaction, and do not assume distance tells you exactly what your advisor thinks. Discuss expectations directly and make your engagement visible through honest, substantive updates.

Slow progress alone does not imply weak commitment. Difficult problems can resist serious work, and students can need preparation or support they have not yet received. Advisors have responsibilities here too: trust requires clear expectations, useful feedback, and a willingness to understand what is blocking progress.

## 7. Be honest about your ambition and commitment

Many students begin by saying they want to explore and discover what interests them. That is a reasonable position. You may have little basis for knowing your interests before experiencing research.

But consider how that statement may be heard by someone deciding whether to invest in a demanding collaboration. They may be unsure whether you want a brief introduction or whether you are prepared to stay engaged through setbacks.

If you want to develop a strong paper or aim for a leading venue, say so. Then support that ambition with consistent work. A concrete goal helps you and your advisor understand the commitment you are trying to build.

Expressing ambition does not guarantee support or publication. It gives the collaboration a clearer purpose.

You can be undecided about your career while committing to a project:

> “I am still figuring out my long-term interests, but I want to take this problem seriously and work toward a publishable contribution.”

There is a real dilemma for students who need exploration before they can make that commitment. Universities and research programs should create opportunities for that stage, with clear expectations and a path into deeper involvement.

At the same time, students should understand that exploratory participation and a sustained effort toward publication ask different things of both the student and the advisor.

## 8. A serious commitment can expand your future choices

Beginners sometimes hold back because they fear choosing the wrong direction. A strong commitment can feel like giving up the right to change their mind.

But carrying a substantial project through to completion can create opportunities that tentative participation does not.

You develop the ability to investigate an unfamiliar problem, interpret evidence, communicate a contribution, and work through uncertainty with others. You build credibility and relationships. You also discover more about what you enjoy and what kind of work suits you.

Those gains remain useful when you change topics.

Committing to a project is not choosing your entire future. It is choosing to engage deeply enough to gain something substantial from the experience.

## 9. A few words as you begin

I wish I had understood these lessons when I started my PhD. They would not have removed the uncertainty, but they could have helped me become more productive and put my research career on a stronger path. I hope sharing them helps you avoid some of the expensive mistakes I made.

Give a promising problem your attention, test the assumptions that matter, and leave room to change your approach. Take initiative, communicate honestly, and work toward a relationship in which you and your advisor can learn from unexpected results together. These habits will serve you through your first project and beyond it.

Research can be frustrating, but it can also bring the satisfaction of understanding something that was previously unclear—and contributing something others can build on. I hope you find a question that holds your curiosity, mentors and collaborators who support your growth, and the confidence to pursue work you will be proud of. Good luck with your first steps into research.

For further inspiration, I recommend Jack E. Oliver’s [*The Incomplete Guide to the Art of Discovery*](https://archive.org/details/incompleteguidet0000oliv/page/n7/mode/2up).
